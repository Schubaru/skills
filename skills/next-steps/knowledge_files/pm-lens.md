# PM lens

Read the meeting the way a product manager would. The question is not "what was said?" It is "who owes what, to whom, by when, and what is stuck?"

## Who owns a step

- The owner is the person the notes name as the doer. A shared heading does not make everyone the owner.
- Sharing something belongs to the sender. If Sam sends the user a recording, "send recording" is Sam's step. "Watch the recording" is the user's.
- Raising a problem does not make you the owner of the fix.
- A step with no clear owner goes under "Questions for you", but only if it could be the user's. Never guess.
- Turn "today", "tomorrow", and "this week" into real days, counted from the meeting date.
- Granola's participant list is often incomplete. A one-on-one call may list only the user. Use names in the title and the notes to tell who was in the room.

## the user's lane

Keep a step for the user when they are the doer or the blocker. That includes design work, flows, prototypes, building or fixing skills, reviews, handoffs, feedback they owe, and decisions waiting on them.

Gray zone: if a step is plausibly theirs but not clearly assigned, ask under "Questions for you". A wrong guess costs more than a question.

Calls and intros where the user only has to show up go under "On your calendar". They're not steps, so they're never ranked and never get a prompt. If they need to prep, the prep is the step.

## Promises made for the user

People often commit the user to something in rooms they aren't in, often a lead or a partner speaking for them.

When someone promises a deliverable, date, or scope on the user's behalf, list it as the user's step and tag it `(<Name> committed)`. Don't use the tag when the user agreed to it themselves in the room. The user reads these differently. They didn't agree, they may need to push back, and they need to know the promise exists.

## Other people's steps

For one meeting, list what each other person owes, kept short. Lead with anything they owe the user, because that's what they'll nudge people about.

For several meetings, or a no-link "what's next?", list only what people owe the user.

## Merging notes

- One meeting can have two Granola notes, for example one from the user and one from a teammate. Same time and overlapping titles usually means same meeting. Read both. Each step shows up once.
- When several meetings are shared, a step that shows up twice is one step. The newer meeting wins.

## Ranking the user's steps

Highest first:

1. Someone is blocked waiting on the user, or a client is waiting.
2. A hard deadline today or tomorrow.
3. A promise someone made for the user that they need to accept or push back on.
4. Money on the line: revenue, a renewal, a new client intro.
5. Everything else, by deadline.

Tie: the smaller job goes first so the queue keeps moving.

The "why #1" note names the rule that won, in plain words. Example shape: "Sam is waiting on this for a client follow-up, and it was due the day of the meeting."

## Learning

Watch for corrections in the chat: the user moves a step up or down, says a step is done, drops a step, rewrites the prompt, or comments on tone or length. They might also say "remember…".

When that happens:

1. Write one short rule per distinct correction that would have prevented the miss. Make it about a pattern, not this one meeting.
2. Skip a rule that's already covered by the defaults or the file. Say which line already covers it.
3. Show the new rules as one diff for the "Learned rules" section of `knowledge_files/learned-preferences.md`, with today's date.
4. Ask: "Save these rules?" with Yes / No / Edit as the choices.
5. On yes in Claude Code, add the lines to the file. In Chat or Cowork, where the file can't be edited, hand them the lines to paste in.
6. If a new rule conflicts with an old one, show both and ask which to keep.

Offer two rules per run at most. Don't store private details about people. Store only how the user likes to work.
